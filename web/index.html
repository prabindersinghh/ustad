The problem

The knowledge leaves before the paperwork arrives.

Changeovers, maintenance, quality checks: the work that decides whether a line runs clean is held in people, not documents. When they retire or move on, it goes with them, and the next worker learns it the expensive way, through scrap, downtime and rework.

Writing it down does not solve it. A written procedure takes weeks to produce, is out of date the first time the line changes, and can tell you what should have happened but never whether it did.

How it works

One recording in. Every shift covered after.

Capture, for the expert

Do the job once, out loud

The expert wears the camera and works at their own pace, explaining as they go. Ustad listens, watches, and writes the procedure: ordered steps, the exact settings and torques they mentioned, the hazard to call out before each one, and, critically, what the camera should see when a step is done right.

Guide, for the worker

Hands free, and checked

The next worker hears one step at a time, hands free. When they say they're done, Ustad looks before it agrees. If the step is right, it advances and banks a photo. If it's wrong, it says so, says why, and holds them there until it's fixed.

The loop

What happens when a worker says "done"

This is the part that is hard, and the part that matters. Anything can read a checklist aloud. Ustad commits to a judgement about the physical world and acts on it.

  1. 1
    See

    The worker's current view is captured the moment they say done.

  2. 2
    Ground

    SAM 3.1 finds the step's target part in that frame and answers with coordinates and a confidence, not an opinion.

  3. 3
    Judge

    Muse Spark weighs the frame, the grounding and the success criteria, and must commit to exactly one action.

  4. 4
    Act

    Advance and bank the evidence, correct the worker, or ask for a clearer view. Guessing is not an option.

Why it's different

Built to be wrong out loud

It refuses to guess

When the camera cannot see enough to judge a step, Ustad says so and asks the worker to reposition. A confident wrong answer on a factory floor is worse than no answer, so it is not an option the system has.

It checks its own procedures

A generated step is only useful if the thing it names can be found. After writing a procedure, Ustad runs the segmentation model back over the expert's own footage to confirm every step names something real and visible, and rewrites the ones that don't.

It writes the audit trail for you

Every judged step leaves a timestamped photo. A finished job produces the record a supervisor would otherwise have to chase, without anyone filling in a form.

The visual evidence decides what happens next.

That is the line between a copilot and a chatbot describing a photograph.

Where it runs

One copilot. Two ways to wear it.
Today

Works on a phone

Strap a phone to your chest and you have a point-of-view camera, a microphone and a speaker: everything the loop needs, with nothing to install and nothing to buy.
Where it's going

Comes alive on smart glasses

Glasses put the camera where the hands are and keep them free. The backend does not change: the camera is an interface, not an assumption.

Your footage can stay on your floor

The first question a plant asks is not about accuracy. It is whether video of their line leaves the site. Ustad runs its verification reasoning on a Meta open-weight model, and every judged step records which engine decided it, so the answer to "where did our footage go" is a log, not a promise.

Proof

Tested on a real line, not a desk

Built against a working paint manufacturer, on procedures their people actually run, with their expert and their equipment. Novices ran the same job twice, once the way the plant does it today and once with Ustad, and we planted errors on purpose to find out what it catches.
